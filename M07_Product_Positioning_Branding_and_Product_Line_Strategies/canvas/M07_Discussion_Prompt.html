Background

Highline Footwear has been a leading mid-price athletic footwear brand for a decade. A new competitor has entered the market at a lower price point and is eroding Highline's share in the value segment. Two executives are looking at the same brand equity scorecard and reaching opposite conclusions about how to respond.

The CFO: Stay the Course

Our brand equity is +36, and it has been stable. Defending against the low-price entrant would require either launching a fighter brand ($3.6M Year 1 cost) or discounting our core line. Neither option makes financial sense when equity is already in our favor. We should stay the course and protect margin.

The Brand Manager: Launch the Fighter Brand

You're reading the scorecard wrong. Yes, equity is +36 now, but our Customer Dissatisfaction score is already Above Average, the highest liability dimension we carry. If that liability keeps rising, equity falls to +24. We're one product failure away from a materially different position. The $3.6M fighter brand is not a luxury: it's the floor of our defense. Waiting is how brands lose equity they can't rebuild.

What “Specific Data” Means

The best posts cite at least two specific values from the table above: Highline's Brand Equity, its Customer Dissatisfaction score, the fighter brand cost, or the ROA comparison. Saying “the liabilities are rising” is not a data-supported argument. Saying “a Customer Dissatisfaction score of 75 is the highest single liability dimension, and if it keeps rising equity falls from +36 to +24” is a data-supported argument. The numbers matter — use the ones from the table, in your own words.

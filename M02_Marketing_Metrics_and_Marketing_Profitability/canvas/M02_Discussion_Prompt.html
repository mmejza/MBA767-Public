Background

Cascadia ActiveWear is a mid-size regional apparel company with $54 million in annual revenue. Its Year 3 performance data has just been reviewed at a senior leadership meeting: Marketing ROS of 17.7% (below the Fortune 500 average of 23.5%), and a Marketing ROI of 75.7%, below the 100% floor a marketing program needs to clear to recover its own cost. The Vice President of Finance and the Vice President of Marketing have reached sharply different conclusions about what to do, both speaking from the same data set.

The VP of Finance's Position

The data is unambiguous. Our Marketing ROI is 75.7%: we are not recovering our marketing investment. My recommendation has three parts. First, reduce marketing and sales expenses by 20% — if MROI is below 100%, the solution is to spend less until the ratio recovers. Second, drop the Outdoor Accessories product line: it loses $180,000 per year, and eliminating it reduces our exposure to an underperforming category. Third, cut core product prices by 10% to grow market share — more volume at a lower price will increase total NMC by driving more customers into the calculation. All three of these actions address the MROI problem directly.

The VP of Marketing's Position

I agree that MROI of 75.7% is a serious problem. But all three of the Finance VP's proposed solutions will make our NMC worse, not better. On the price cut: a 10% reduction compresses gross margin significantly, and App 1 shows the added volume does not compensate — NMC falls, not rises. On the Outdoor Accessories line: the Finance VP is looking at net profit, not NMC. The line generates +$0.30M in NMC; drop it and total NMC falls by exactly that amount. The MROI problem is real, but it requires fixing the gross margin structure, not cutting the programs that are actually generating positive marketing profit.

What "Specific Data" Means

The best posts cite at least two specific numbers from your own work in Apps 1–3, not the background table above: App 1's before-and-after NMC for the price cut, App 2's total-NMC-before-and-after for the line drop, or App 3's diagnosis of whether 75.7% is a floor problem or just a below-average result. Saying "the price cut seems risky" is not a data-supported argument. Saying "App 1's own NMC output falls from $9.54M to $8.1M under the 10% price cut, since the margin compression outweighs the added volume" is a data-supported argument. The numbers matter.

A Note on the Right Answer

There is a defensible data-based answer here: working through each of the Finance VP's three proposals with Apps 1–3's own NMC math reveals which ones actually hold up.

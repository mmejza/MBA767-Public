Background

Solstice Industrial Components sells precision fasteners through two channels: a direct sales force and an independent distributor network. The VP of Sales has brought a proposal to the leadership team: shift 20 percentage points of volume from the distributor channel to direct sales, and increase direct-channel marketing and selling expense from $8.4M to $16.8M annually. The CFO has pushed back. Both are using module concepts to make their case.

The VP of Sales's Position

This is straightforward. Direct sales delivers our highest per-unit margin by a wide margin over the distributor channel: every point of volume we shift from distributors to direct captures profit we're currently handing to intermediaries. The $8.4M increase in direct marketing and selling expense is small next to the contribution direct sales already generates. We should approve the full 20-point shift and stop leaving margin on the table.

The CFO's Position

Marketing contribution dollars aren't the same as marketing efficiency. Direct sales already carries by far our largest cost base: a sales force that individually acquires and services every account. Before we lock in a 100% increase in direct spend, I want to see what we're actually getting back per dollar invested, not just which channel shows the biggest number on the P&L. A channel can generate more profit and still be the worse place to put the next marginal dollar.

What "Specific Data" Means

The best posts cite at least two specific concepts from the module: pocket price by channel, Net Marketing Contribution, Marketing ROS, Marketing ROI, or the channel performance triad from Chapter 9. Saying "direct sales is more profitable" is not a data-supported argument. Saying "a channel's Marketing ROI depends on the ratio of NMC to M&S expense, so a channel with higher absolute NMC can still show a lower ROI if its expense base is proportionally larger" is a data-supported argument. The mechanism matters, not just the direction.
